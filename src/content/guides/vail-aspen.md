Aspen and Vail attract couples for different reasons. Aspen is the Maroon Bells, Independence Pass and a town that still looks like a silver camp. Vail is a built-for-skiing village with lift-served ceremony decks and a quieter valley of ranches and passes. Both are expensive, both are spectacular, and both reward the couple who understands the reservation calendar before they fall in love with a date.

## Why elope here

You come to Aspen for the most recognizable backdrop in the state, the Maroon Bells over Maroon Lake, and the Forest Service has turned the ceremony question into a straightforward booking, which is rare. You come to Vail for infrastructure: resorts that have spent decades perfecting small weddings with a gondola ride to the altar, plus a quiet valley of passes and a state park when you want something wilder.

## Best ceremony spots

**Maroon Bells Amphitheater.** A tucked-away stone amphitheater above the main trails at Maroon Lake, reserved through Recreation.gov. The site is yours from 8 a.m. to 3 p.m. on the day you book, then becomes first come. The lake is public and crowded; the amphitheater is private enough. Your reservation includes five vehicle passes up Maroon Creek Road, which is otherwise closed to private cars from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. in season. Everyone else rides the shuttle from Aspen Highlands.

**Independence Pass.** Highway 82 crests the Continental Divide at 12,095 feet east of Aspen, with a paved summit pullout and a short trail onto the tundra. Nothing to book, no fee, no services. The pass closes from roughly early November until late May, and storms can close it any September afternoon. A tiny ceremony with no props falls under general Forest Service rules; call the Aspen-Sopris Ranger District for anything larger.

**Ashcroft Ghost Town.** Eleven miles up Castle Creek Road, preserved 1880s buildings in a meadow below the Elk Range. The Aspen Historical Society manages the site and handles event bookings, so it is not a show-up-and-marry spot, but it is the most atmospheric alternative when the amphitheater is full.

**Shrine Ridge, Vail Pass.** A moderate four-mile round trip from the Shrine Pass trailhead through July wildflowers to a ridge facing Mount of the Holy Cross. Dispersed national forest with no reservation system, so keep it small. The road is seasonal; in winter it is a snowmobile route with a day fee.

**Vail Mountain and Beaver Creek decks.** The resorts operate ceremony sites reached by gondola, with catering and a weather plan, booked through the resort's wedding office. The price reflects it. For something quieter, Sylvan Lake State Park south of Eagle has a lake, cabins and yurts, with ceremonies arranged through the park.

## Permits and rules

The Maroon Bells Amphitheater is booked on [Recreation.gov](https://www.recreation.gov/camping/campgrounds/233123), where reservations open one year ahead at 8 a.m. Mountain time on a rolling basis. The Aspen Chamber lists the rate at $200 per day before taxes and fees; the Forest Service sets it. The amphitheater is not reservable on Fridays and Saturdays in June, July and August, or Fridays through Sundays in September, which is the single fact that reshapes most couples' plans. Arches, thrown petals, released animals, PA systems and hanging lights are prohibited, and everything you bring must leave with you. Receptions cannot happen at the amphitheater; the East Maroon Portal picnic site down the road reserves for up to 70 people. The Chamber's [Maroon Bells wedding page](https://aspenchamber.org/groups/weddings/maroon-bells/) collects the rules, shuttle fares and contacts in one place. Pitkin County is slated to take over operations of the scenic area in 2027 with higher day-use fees, so re-check the listing when you book.

Elsewhere on the White River National Forest, non-commercial groups under 75 people without props generally need no permit, while chairs, arches or paid vendors on site may trigger one; the [Aspen-Sopris Ranger District](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r02/whiteriver/recreation/aspen-sopris-ranger-district) and the Holy Cross district in Minturn answer. State parks require a special activity agreement; see [Colorado Parks and Wildlife](https://cpw.state.co.us/weddings-and-special-events).

## Your marriage license

Any Colorado county clerk can issue a license that works statewide. You both appear with photo ID (a notarized absentee affidavit covers a partner who cannot), the fee is $30, there is no waiting period, blood test or witness requirement, and under C.R.S. 14-2-109 you may solemnize your own marriage. Use the license within 35 days and return the signed certificate to the issuing clerk within 63 days of the ceremony.

The Pitkin County Clerk and Recorder is on Main Street in Aspen. The Eagle County Clerk has offices in Eagle and El Jebel (its Avon office closed in 2026), so Vail couples drive to Eagle and Basalt couples use El Jebel. Statewide rules are spelled out on any clerk's page, such as [Larimer County's](https://www.larimer.gov/clerk/recording/marriage).

## When to go

**December through April** is ski season. Maroon Creek Road is closed beyond the T-Lazy-7 Ranch, so the Bells are a six-mile ski or snowshoe, Independence Pass is closed, and holiday lodging peaks. Resort decks shine in this season; our [winter elopement guide](/resources/winter-elopement-colorado) covers closures and driving rules.

**May** is mud and melt: the Bells road usually reopens in mid May, the pass around Memorial Day, and the lake is often still iced in.

**June** is green, with snow on the peaks and the first wildflowers at Maroon Lake late in the month. Weekend blackouts begin.

**July and August** bring wildflowers on Shrine Ridge and the pass, afternoon thunderstorms most days by 1 p.m., and the busiest trails of the year. Book sunrise and be off the mountain by noon.

**Late September** is why the Bells are famous: the aspens below the peaks turn gold in the last ten days of the month, the air is dry, and weekend reservations are impossible, so aim for a Tuesday.

**October** is crisp and quiet until the road closes to cars, typically by late October, and the pass follows in early November.

## Sunrise or sunset, and a sample day

The Bells face east, so sunrise is the shot: alpenglow on the peaks, a still lake, the amphitheater before the shuttle crowds. Your reservation starts at 8 a.m., well after first light in September, so many couples do first-look portraits at the lake at dawn and the ceremony at 8:15. Independence Pass is a sunset site in good weather and a sunrise site when storms are forecast.

A late-September Tuesday at the Bells:

1. 5:00 a.m. Hair and makeup in Aspen.
2. 6:15 a.m. Up Maroon Creek Road with your vehicle passes, before the 8 a.m. gate.
3. 6:50 a.m. First light on the peaks. Portraits along the lake.
4. 8:15 a.m. Ceremony in the amphitheater.
5. 9:30 a.m. Breakfast in town, or a picnic at East Maroon Portal if reserved.
6. 5:30 p.m. Independence Pass for sunset, sky permitting.

## Where to stay

Aspen puts you fifteen minutes from the Bells and walking distance to dinner, at the highest prices in Colorado. Snowmass Village is slightly cheaper with the same access, and Basalt and Carbondale, 25 to 40 minutes down valley, are the local choice for value. On the Vail side, Vail Village and Lionshead are the walkable core, Avon and Edwards are more affordable, Minturn has small-town character, and Eagle is the budget base near the airport.

## Getting there

Aspen/Pitkin County Airport is fifteen minutes from town, with weather delays a known risk. Eagle County Regional in Gypsum is 35 minutes to Vail and about an hour and 15 minutes to Aspen via Glenwood Springs. From Denver International, Vail is two hours on I-70 and Aspen is three and a half to four via Glenwood, or over Independence Pass in summer, with hairpins no trailer should attempt.
