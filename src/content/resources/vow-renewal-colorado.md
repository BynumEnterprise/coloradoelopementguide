A vow renewal is the easiest Colorado ceremony to plan on paper and exactly as hard as an elopement on the ground. There is no license to buy and no certificate to return, because you are not creating a new marriage. But a ranger at a trailhead does not care whether you are marrying or renewing. If you stand in a national park, a state park or a city park and hold a ceremony, the same land rules apply to you as to any eloping couple. This guide covers both halves, plus the practical parts: who leads the ceremony, how to bring kids, and what a photographer costs.

## Do you need a Colorado marriage license?

No. A Colorado marriage license is how two unmarried people enter a legal marriage, and you have already done that, whether in Colorado, another state or another country. A renewal is a ceremony, not a legal event, so nothing gets filed with a county clerk afterward and your original marriage certificate stays the record that matters.

There is one wrinkle worth knowing. Some couples want a Colorado record, for example because they married by common law or simply want a fresh certificate. Park County's clerk says on its marriage license page that a couple already married who wish to renew their vows, or who have had a common law marriage, may obtain a license by stating that they are married to each other. That is one county's published practice, not a statewide rule, so if you want a license for a renewal, call the clerk in the county you plan to use and ask before you go. If you do get one, the normal rules apply: $30, valid 35 days anywhere in Colorado, and the certificate goes back to the clerk. Our [Colorado marriage license guide](/resources/colorado-marriage-license) covers those details.

## The land rules still apply

This is where vow renewals catch couples off guard. Permits are about the activity on the land, not your legal status.

| Where | Rule that applies to a renewal | Our guide |
|-------|--------------------------------|-----------|
| Rocky Mountain National Park | Special Use Permit required, $300 fee, designated sites only | [RMNP permit](/resources/rocky-mountain-national-park-wedding-permit) |
| National forest | Permit needed at 75 or more people, if commercial, or with props | [Forest and BLM](/resources/national-forest-and-blm-elopements) |
| Colorado state parks | Special Activity Agreement may be required; check the park | [State parks](/resources/colorado-state-parks-elopements) |
| Garden of the Gods | No charge at six designated areas, first come, small and brief | [Garden of the Gods](/resources/garden-of-the-gods-elopement) |

**Rocky Mountain National Park** is explicit. Its weddings page says a Special Use Permit is required for weddings, elopements, vow renewals and other ceremonies. The permit carries a $300 non refundable administrative fee, ceremonies happen only at designated sites, every site has a group limit topping out at 30 people, and the two hour window includes setup and photos. Applications open one year ahead of your month, and the summer and fall months fill fast, so an anniversary date in September needs planning a year out.

**National forest land**, which covers most of the famous overlooks, uses the same thresholds for a renewal as for a wedding. The Arapaho and Roosevelt National Forests say a group of 75 or more people needs a permit, commercial events need a permit, and props such as chairs, tents and tables typically need one. Two of you, your kids and a photographer standing at an overlook fall well under the line.

**State parks** are less uniform. Colorado Parks and Wildlife says a Special Activity Agreement or photography permit may be required for an event or group activity and sends you to the specific park's page. Treat your renewal as an event and ask the park.

**Garden of the Gods** in Colorado Springs has six designated ceremony areas with no charge, first come first served, for small and brief ceremonies. The city's Weddings in Parks page also says that more than 50 people in a city park means renting a pavilion or applying for a Special Event Permit.

## Who can officiate

Anyone. Because nothing is being filed, your officiant has no legal role. Couples ask a grown child, a best friend, the person who officiated the first time, or nobody at all and read their vows to each other. A hired [officiant](/category/officiants) still earns their fee when you want someone to write and run the ceremony; a simple ceremony typically runs $300 to $500 (estimate).

If you do get a license for the renewal, the normal Colorado rules apply again, including the option to solemnize it yourselves. See [self solemnization](/resources/self-solemnization-colorado).

## Anniversary ideas that work in Colorado

- **Return to the place.** If you honeymooned in Estes Park or Breckenridge, a sunrise renewal at the same overlook gives the photos a story.
- **Match the season of your wedding.** A fall wedding becomes a renewal in the aspens; our [fall elopement guide](/resources/colorado-fall-elopement) covers when color usually peaks.
- **Rewrite your vows.** Ten or twenty years in, you know what you promised and what you actually did. Couples often read the original vows and then the new ones.
- **Add a small dinner.** A mountain town restaurant with a private room turns a short ceremony into a celebration without a venue rental.
- **Bring the dog.** Rules vary by land manager, so check before you plan around a pet.

## Bringing your kids

Kids are the most common reason couples renew in the mountains instead of at home. Count them in every group limit, because RMNP counts everyone, infants included. Plan around nap and snack windows, keep hikes short, and give older kids a job, such as holding the rings or reading a passage. Our guide to [eloping with kids and dogs](/resources/eloping-with-kids-and-dogs) has packing lists and timing advice that apply just as well to a renewal.

## Photographer session length and cost

The photographer is usually the biggest line on a renewal budget. Typical Colorado ranges, consistent with our [elopement cost guide](/resources/colorado-elopement-cost):

- **One to two hour session** at a single overlook: about $1,200 to $2,500 (estimate).
- **Two to three hours** with a ceremony, family photos and portraits: about $2,500 to $4,000 (estimate).
- **Full day** with a hike, the ceremony and dinner: about $4,500 to $7,000 (estimate).

Ask whether the price includes travel to your region, how many edited images you receive, and whether family groupings with the kids are part of the plan. Our [photography pricing guide](/resources/elopement-photography-pricing-colorado) explains what drives these numbers.
